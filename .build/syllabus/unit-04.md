## Proposed activities

### 1. `shape-sorter-3d` — 3D Shape Sorter 🧊
- **section**: 4.1
- **summary**: Drag everyday objects into labelled boxes or a Venn diagram by curved/straight, face shapes and number of corners.
- **book**: Textbook p.56 Getting started Q1–2, p.57, p.59 Q1, p.60 Q2 · Workbook p.55 Q4, p.59 Q9
- **Learn screens**
  1. *What makes a 3D shape?* A slowly turning cereal box: click a face (it lights up), an edge (glows as a line) and a vertex (a dot pops). Bolt labels each one as it is clicked.
  2. *Flat or curved?* A tin and a dice side by side. The child rolls each one along a shelf (drag) to see the tin rolls and the dice does not; Bolt says the tin has a curved surface.
  3. *Two rules at once.* A 2-circle Venn diagram ("has a curved surface", "has more than 5 corners"). Bolt drops a ball, a box, a tin, a party hat and a pyramid-shaped tea bag in turn. Nothing fits both circles, so Bolt shows that the overlap can be empty, and explains the outside space (the tea bag, with 5 corners, goes outside).
- **Practice (8)**
  - Level 1: drag 4–6 household objects (tin, dice, gift box, ball, party hat, cereal box) into three boxes: curved edges / straight edges / no edges (the ball).
  - Level 2: three labels where some objects go in more than one box (one or more curved surfaces / all faces rectangles / more than five corners); tick-boxes per object. Vertex counts 0–8.
  - Level 3: 2-circle Venn with six shapes and given labels (e.g. "has a square face", "has a triangle face"); objects with neither go outside. Final question: "Name a shape with fewer than 6 corners" (choose from pictures).
- **Mistake tags**: `cylinder-counted-as-straight`, `more-than-vs-at-least`, `missed-overlap`, `forgot-outside`, `face-shape-not-solid`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Sort 3D shapes by what they look like: curved or flat, the shape of their faces and how many corners they have.
  - say: "Would it roll? Then it has a curved surface." · "Touch each corner as you count so you don't count one twice." · "Can it go in both circles? Then it belongs in the middle."
  - watch: Counting hidden corners twice or not at all. · Thinking 5 corners counts as "more than five". · Leaving a shape out when it fits neither label (it goes outside the circles).
  - away from screen: Collect 6 things from the kitchen and sort them on the table into "rolls" and "doesn't roll", then make up your own rule.

### 2. `prism-or-pyramid` — Prism or Pyramid? 🔺
- **section**: 4.1
- **summary**: Check two matching ends and flat faces to decide prism, pyramid or neither, even when shapes are turned over.
- **book**: Textbook p.58 Worked example 1, p.62–63 Q5, p.68 Check your progress Q2–3 · Workbook p.51 Worked example, p.52 Q1, p.54 Q3, p.56 Q5
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Prism test.* A chocolate-bar box (triangular prism) is sliced like bread: every slice is the same triangle. Bolt: a prism has two matching ends and all flat faces. The child clicks both ends to highlight them.
  2. *Pyramid test.* A pyramid-shaped paperweight: the child drags a point up from a square base and the sides fold to meet at the apex. Bolt: one base, triangle sides, all meeting at the apex.
  3. *Neither.* A tin has two matching circle ends but a curved side, so it fails the "all flat faces" test. A cone and a ball also fail. The child taps the failed rule on a checklist.
  4. *Turned around.* The same prism lies on its side and the child rotates it with a slider until the ends face front.
- **Practice (10)**
  - Level 1: one shape at a time (cube, cuboid, triangular prism, square-based pyramid, cylinder, cone, sphere), tap Prism / Pyramid / Neither.
  - Level 2: include pentagonal, hexagonal and octagonal prisms and pyramids, some lying down or upside down (as in the workbook: a pyramid tipped on its side, a cylinder lying down); tap all the prisms on a board of 8–14 drawings. Then name each prism after its end shape (triangular, pentagonal, hexagonal, octagonal), accepting the workbook's other names: 'square prism' for a cube, 'rectangular prism' for a cuboid.
  - Level 3: "Why not?" pick the reason for a non-prism (ends don't match / has a curved surface / comes to a point); and the odd one out from four shapes.
- **Mistake tags**: `cylinder-is-prism`, `cone-is-pyramid`, `missed-turned-shape`, `cube-not-prism`, `only-triangle-prisms`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Tell prisms from pyramids: prisms have two matching ends and flat faces; pyramids rise to one point.
  - say: "Find the two ends. Are they the same shape and size?" · "Are all the faces flat?" · "Turn it round in your head: which end could it stand on?"
  - watch: Calling a tin a prism (it has a curved side). · Missing prisms that are lying on their side. · Not counting a cube or a cereal box as a prism.
  - away from screen: Look for prisms in the cupboard (boxes, a triangular chocolate box, a tent-shaped tea box) and stand each one on its end.

### 3. `face-edge-vertex-count` — Faces, Edges, Corners 🎲
- **section**: 4.1
- **summary**: Click every face, edge and vertex on a turnable 3D shape to count them, then use the counts to solve clue riddles.
- **book**: Textbook p.62 Q4, p.63 Q6, p.64 Q7 dice key, p.61 Q3 · Workbook p.53 Q2, p.58 Q8
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Counting faces.* A dice that can be turned with drag. Each click paints a face; a counter shows 1–6, including the hidden faces, which appear as you turn it.
  2. *Counting edges.* The same dice as a wire frame: click edges and they turn orange (12). Bolt's tip: count the top ring, the bottom ring, then the upright ones (4 + 4 + 4).
  3. *Counting vertices.* Click corners and they get a dot (8). Count the top 4 and then the bottom 4.
- **Practice (10)**
  - Level 1: cube, cuboid, square-based pyramid: count faces (5–6) and vertices (5–8) by clicking.
  - Level 2: triangular prism (5 faces, 9 edges, 6 vertices), hexagonal prism (8, 18, 12), triangle-based pyramid (4, 6, 4), cylinder/cone/sphere (vertices 0 or 1; curved surfaces). Fill in a properties card (name, prism/pyramid/neither, faces, edges, vertices). Stretch item from Workbook p.58: octagonal prism (10, 24, 16), counted on a turnable model rather than imagined.
  - Level 3: riddle cards: "I have 8 faces", "I have no corners", "My faces are triangles and one square", "I have a curved surface and a circle face". Pick from 5 pictures; then build your own riddle by choosing three property tiles for Bolt to guess.
- **Mistake tags**: `counted-twice`, `missed-hidden`, `edges-vs-vertices`, `curved-surface-as-face`, `wrong-name`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Count faces, edges and corners (vertices) of 3D shapes carefully and use them to identify a shape.
  - say: "Count the top ring, then the bottom ring, then the bits that join them." · "Turn it to see the back. Are there hidden faces?" · "Edges are the lines, vertices are the corners."
  - watch: Counting a corner twice when turning the shape. · Mixing up edges and corners. · Forgetting the bottom face. · The workbook (p.56–57, p.58) asks for counts with no picture, and calls a tin's curved side a 'face' in one place and a 'surface' in another: let the child build or hold the shape, and accept either word.
  - away from screen: Use a sticker or a pen dot on each corner of a real box as you count, and a strip of tape along each edge.

### 4. `cube-builder` — Cube Builder 🟧
- **section**: 4.1 (Think like a mathematician)
- **summary**: Build cuboids from cubes layer by layer and count how many cubes they use, then find all the cuboids for a number.
- **book**: Textbook p.66 Think like a mathematician; p.65 Q8 (two cubes joined) · Workbook p.58 Q7, p.59 Q10 (row of 4 linking cubes)
- **Learn screens**
  1. *One layer.* A tray: the child drags sugar-cube style blocks into a 3 by 2 layer and counts 6 (count in rows: 3, 6).
  2. *Stack the layers.* Press "add layer" twice: 6, 12, 18. Bolt shows "3 layers of 6 = 18" alongside "6 + 6 + 6 = 18".
  3. *Hidden cubes.* The model turns to show the cubes at the back that were hidden.
- **Practice (8)**
  - Level 1: count cubes in a pictured cuboid that can be turned (8, 12 or 16 cubes; one or two layers), using layer counting.
  - Level 2: copy a pictured model by building it (2x2x2 = 8, 2x4x2 = 16, 3x2x3 = 18, 6x1x3 = 18) and say how many cubes it uses; notice two different shapes can use 18.
  - Level 3: "Use exactly 12 cubes": build as many different cuboids as you can (1x1x12, 1x2x6, 1x3x4, 2x2x3); a tick list shows which have been found and nudges the child to work in order.
- **Mistake tags**: `missed-hidden-cubes`, `counted-front-only`, `same-shape-turned`, `not-systematic`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Count cubes in a cuboid by counting one layer and adding the layers, and find different cuboids with the same number of cubes.
  - say: "How many in the bottom layer? How many layers?" · "Count in sixes: 6, 12, 18." · "Is this really new, or the same one turned round?"
  - watch: Counting only the cubes you can see. · Thinking a turned cuboid is a new one. · Losing count on big models (encourage layer-by-layer).
  - away from screen: Build towers and blocks from Lego bricks or sugar cubes and count them layer by layer.

### 5. `straw-shape-builder` — Straws and Clay 🥤
- **section**: Project 2 (also 4.1 Q9)
- **summary**: Join straws with clay balls to build skeleton prisms, then change each prism into a pyramid and record the counts.
- **book**: Textbook p.67 Q9, p.69–70 Project 2
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Straws are edges, balls are corners.* The child drags straws between clay balls to complete a triangular prism skeleton (9 straws, 6 balls); counters tick up.
  2. *Prism to pyramid.* Bolt removes the top triangle (3 straws, 3 balls), adds one ball above, and the child drags the three upright straws to meet it: 6 straws, 4 balls.
  3. *Fill the table.* The counts drop into a table row for "3-sided base".
- **Practice (8)**
  - Level 1: count straws and balls on a finished skeleton (cube 12/8, triangular prism 9/6, square-based pyramid 8/5).
  - Level 2: build a square-based prism and turn it into a pyramid; fill in the table (12/8 -> 8/5).
  - Level 3: predict before building for 5- and 6-sided bases (15/10 -> 10/6; 18/12 -> 12/7), then check by building. Spot the pattern: the prism's straw count goes up by 3 each time; the pyramid's by 2.
- **Mistake tags**: `double-counted-ball`, `forgot-apex`, `missed-back-edge`, `pattern-not-seen`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Build prisms and pyramids from straws and clay and notice how the counts of edges and corners change.
  - say: "Each straw is one edge, each ball is one corner." · "What did we take away? What did we add?" · "The table goes 9, 12, 15... what comes next?"
  - watch: Counting a ball twice where several straws meet. · Forgetting the pyramid needs one new ball at the top. · Guessing the next number instead of building to check.
  - away from screen: Make a triangular prism and a pyramid from cocktail sticks (or straws) and play dough, then count and compare.

### 6. `mystery-shape-clues` — Mystery Shape 🕵️
- **section**: 4.1 (end-of-unit review)
- **summary**: Bolt hides a 3D shape in a box and gives clues one at a time; the child crosses out shapes until one is left.
- **book**: Textbook p.61 Q3, p.62 Q4, p.64 Q7 dice game, p.68 Check your progress · Workbook p.56–57 Q6 riddles
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Crossing out.* A board of 8 shape pictures. Clue 1 "I have no curved surfaces" fades out the ball, tin and cone. The child taps to confirm.
  2. *Keep going.* Clue 2 "I have a triangle face" and clue 3 "I have 5 corners" leave one shape (square-based pyramid). Bolt opens the box.
- **Practice (8)**
  - Level 1: 2-clue riddles about curved/flat and face shape, with 4–6 pictures.
  - Level 2: 3-clue riddles using counts (faces 5–8, corners 0–8) and prism/pyramid words, with 8 pictures.
  - Level 3: dice-key game against Bolt: roll a property (symmetrical, all faces identical, has a triangle/circle/rectangle face, all faces squares) and claim a matching shape on a 16-shape board; most claims wins.
- **Mistake tags**: `misread-clue`, `crossed-out-wrong`, `property-confusion`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Use shape properties as clues to name a 3D shape, and describe a shape without saying its name.
  - say: "Which shapes can we cross out now?" · "Describe it to me without saying its name." · "Is it a prism, a pyramid or neither?"
  - watch: Jumping to a guess after the first clue. · Mixing up 'faces' and 'corners' in clues.
  - away from screen: Play "I'm thinking of a shape" with real objects: take turns giving one clue at a time.
