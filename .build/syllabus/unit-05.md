# Unit 5 Multiplication and division: syllabus notes (paraphrased; do not copy book text)

Source: Cambridge Primary Mathematics Learner's Book 3 (2nd ed.), printed pages 71–88.
Scan images read: index 75–92 (offset +4 held throughout; printed page numbers checked on every page).
Workbook 3 pages 60–73 also read (index = printed page + 2); see "## Workbook" below.
Sections: 5.1 Exploring multiplication and division (p.73–77 top), 5.2 Connecting 2×, 4× and 8× (p.77–81),
5.3 Connecting 3×, 6× and 9× (p.82–88, with the unit Check your progress on p.87–88). No project follows Unit 5.

**Learner note:** this is the child's weakest area. The book moves fast: it assumes the 1, 2, 5 and 10 tables are
known (Getting started) and then builds 4×, 8×, 3×, 6× and 9× by doubling, halving and adding. Every proposed
activity therefore starts from a picture (equal groups, arrays, number lines) and shows repeated addition before
the × sign, and keeps division as "how many groups?" / "how many in each group?" alongside the array.
Note that the book itself has very little division practice in this unit (fact families only); division is
expanded in Unit 11.

### Workbook: hidden prerequisites and traps (Unit 5)

- **Zero:** 0 × 10 (5.1 Q12) and 3 × 0, 6 × 0, 9 × 0 (5.3 Q9) appear, but the textbook never multiplies by zero.
- **Inequality signs:** the riddles in 5.1 Q6 and Q9 use > and <, which Unit 5 does not teach.
- **Square facts:** 5.1 Q7 and Q10 need the idea that n × n has only four facts in its family. The textbook never mentions this.
- **Ordinal wording:** "the third / 7th / fifth / 9th multiple of …" (5.2 Q6, Q9) is new language. Read it as "4 × 3", "4 × 7" and so on.
- **Beyond × 10:** the workbook goes past the tables: multiples of 4 after 40 (5.2 Q7), and 102–120 as multiples of 3 and 6 (5.3 Q10).
- **Counting back with a "subtract" rule:** 5.3 Q11 counts back in 9s, but the textbook only uses "add" rules.
- **Tables before they are taught:** the 5.2 Challenge grid (Q12) needs 3×, 6× and 9× facts before 5.3 teaches them.
- **Contexts the textbook does not use:** money (c and $) and length (cm, m) in 5.1 Q11 and 5.2 Q13–Q15, and 5.3 Q7.
- **Hidden fact:** spider legs (5.2 Q4) assume the child knows a spider has 8 legs, because the picture is too small to count them.
- **Division is still thin:** apart from fact families (5.1 Q3), the workbook has no division practice in this unit.

## Proposed activities

All activities in this unit use the same three visual bridges, so the child sees one consistent picture of multiplication:
**equal groups** (things in bags, boxes, plates), **arrays** (rows and columns that can be turned), and **number-line jumps**.
Every × sentence appears together with the repeated addition it stands for (e.g. "6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24" under "6 × 4 = 24")
until the child is fluent. Unit 5 gets 7 activities because it is three textbook sections, 18 pages and the child's weakest area.

### 1. `equal-groups-maker` — Equal Groups 🍎
- **section**: 5.1 (unit introduction p.72)
- **summary**: Put the same number of things in each bag, count by groups, and turn "groups of" into a × sentence.
- **book**: Textbook p.71 Getting started Q1–2, p.72 (8 teams of 6), p.76 Q8 · Workbook p.65 Q1 and Q4, p.68 Q13–14, p.69 Q15, p.71 Q7
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Same in every group.* Four plates and a bowl of apples. The child drags apples so each plate has 3. Bolt says "4 groups of 3". If one plate has a different number, Bolt says the groups are not equal yet.
  2. *Count by groups.* Each plate lights up in turn and a number line jumps 3, 6, 9, 12 above it. Sentence builder: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12.
  3. *The × shortcut.* The repeated addition morphs into 3 × 4 = 12, with labels "group size" under 3 and "number of groups" under 4 (the book's order).
  4. *Back the other way (division).* 12 apples, "put 3 on each plate: how many plates?" The child drags into groups of 3; Bolt writes 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
- **Practice (10)**
  - Level 1: make and count equal groups of 2, 5 or 10 (up to 6 groups) with pictures: socks in pairs, fingers on hands, pencils in boxes of 10. Answer the total, then choose the matching × sentence from two.
  - Level 2: groups of 3 or 4 (up to 8 groups): eggs in egg cups, chair legs. Type the repeated addition and the × sentence. Includes "12 cakes, 4 on each plate: how many plates?" (division as grouping) with totals up to 40.
  - Level 3: groups of 6, 8 or 9 (up to 8 groups), e.g. teams of 6 players, spiders with 8 legs (the legs are drawn and countable, and Bolt says "a spider has 8 legs", because the workbook assumes this), price and length stories from the workbook (7 pears at 8c, 8 T-shirts at $4, six 4 m planks, 7 jumps of 9 m) shown as equal bars; plus "boxes of 10" problems with 2-digit numbers of boxes (e.g. 14 boxes of 10 = 140).
- **Mistake tags**: `unequal-groups`, `counted-in-ones`, `size-vs-groups-swap`, `added-instead`, `lost-count`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Understand that multiplying means adding equal groups: group size × number of groups.
  - say: "How many in each group? How many groups?" · "Count in threes with me: 3, 6, 9…" · "Is every group the same? Then we can multiply."
  - watch: Counting every single object instead of counting in groups. · Muddling which number is the group size. · Making groups that are not all the same size.
  - away from screen: Set the table: 4 plates with 2 biscuits each. Ask "how many biscuits?" and say it as "4 groups of 2".

### 2. `array-fact-family` — Array Fact Family 🟡
- **section**: 5.1
- **summary**: Build an array of counters, turn it round, and collect all four × and ÷ facts that belong to it.
- **book**: Textbook p.75 Q4–Q5, p.87 Check your progress Q1, p.71 Getting started Q2 · Workbook p.61 Q3, p.63–64 Q7 and Q10
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Rows and columns.* An egg tray / muffin tin. The child drags to set 3 rows of 5. Rows light up one at a time with jumps 5, 10, 15: "3 rows of 5 = 15".
  2. *Turn it round.* A "rotate" button turns the tin a quarter turn: now 5 rows of 3, still 15. Bolt: "Turn-around facts (commutative): 5 × 3 = 3 × 5."
  3. *Sharing it out.* Slide a divider between rows: "15 split into 3 rows: 5 in each row", 15 ÷ 3 = 5; then between columns: 15 ÷ 5 = 3.
  4. *The family triangle.* 3, 5 and 15 go on a triangle card with the big number at the top. The four facts appear; Bolt points out the big number is always the one being divided.
- **Practice (10)**
  - Level 1: arrays from the 2, 5 and 10 tables (up to 5 rows): count the array, then drag number tiles into the 2 × and 2 ÷ facts.
  - Level 2: arrays from the 3, 4 and 6 tables up to 6 rows (e.g. 6 rows of 5 = 30, 4 rows of 6 = 24): fill all 8 statements including "30 = 6 × 5" and "5 = 30 ÷ 6" forms.
  - Level 3: spot-the-mistake: Bolt writes a family with 1–2 wrong facts (e.g. "30 = 10 ÷ 3"); the child taps the wrong ones and fixes them. Also build-your-own array for a given fact (9 rows of 6 = 54). Square arrays (2 × 2, 5 × 5): turning them gives the same picture, so the family has only 4 facts (Workbook p.63 Q7).
- **Mistake tags**: `division-flipped`, `total-in-wrong-place`, `not-commutative`, `miscounted-array`, `missing-fact`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: See that one array gives two multiplication facts and two division facts (a fact family).
  - say: "How many rows? How many in each row?" · "Turn it round. Has the number of counters changed?" · "Which number is the biggest? That's the one we divide."
  - watch: Writing the division backwards (5 ÷ 30). · Thinking 3 × 5 and 5 × 3 are different amounts. · Counting every counter one by one instead of row by row.
  - away from screen: Lay out coins or buttons in 3 rows of 4 on a tray, turn the tray round, and say the four facts together.

### 3. `multiples-hunter` — Multiples Hunter 🔍
- **section**: 5.1 (with 5.2 Q5, 5.3 Q4)
- **summary**: Find multiples of 2, 5 and 10 by their last digit, sort them in a Venn diagram, and light up 4s, 8s and 9s on a number grid.
- **book**: Textbook p.73 Q1–2, p.74 Q3 and Think like a mathematician 1, p.79 Q5, p.80 reflection, p.83 Q4, p.88 Check your progress Q2 · Workbook p.60 Worked example and Q1, p.61 Q2, p.62 Q5–Q6, p.63 Q9, p.65 Q3, p.67 Q6–Q9
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Last digit clues.* A row of house doors numbered 1–30. Counting in 2s lights doors 2, 4, 6…; Bolt highlights the ones digits 0, 2, 4, 6, 8: "even = a multiple of 2". The same for 5s (0 or 5) and 10s (0).
  2. *Big numbers too.* Bolt covers all but the last digit of 532 and 95: "Only the ones digit decides."
  3. *Venn sort.* Two circles: "multiple of 5", "multiple of 10". Bolt drops 45, 120 and 96 in and shows why the "10 only" part stays empty: every multiple of 10 is also a multiple of 5.
- **Practice (10)**
  - Level 1: tap the multiples of 2 (or 5, or 10) among 8–12 numbers up to 100, then up to 1000.
  - Level 2: drag 8–12 numbers up to 1000 into a Venn diagram (multiple of 5 / multiple of 10, or multiple of 2 / multiple of 5) including the outside region; then "which part is empty?"
  - Level 3: colour multiples of 4 or 8 on a 71–120 grid and multiples of 9 on a 100 square, building them by jumping (a frog hops 8 squares at a time). Then tick the true descriptions (all even / odd and even mixed / digits add to 9). Workbook-style riddles: "I am odd, between 70 and 80, and a multiple of 5" (shown in words first, then with the > and < signs side by side, since Unit 5 doesn't teach them); "what is the 7th multiple of 4?" answered by counting 7 hops.
- **Mistake tags**: `first-digit-checked`, `five-means-ten`, `missed-overlap`, `forgot-outside`, `skipped-multiple`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Recognise multiples of 2, 5 and 10 from the last digit and use the words multiple, odd and even.
  - say: "Cover up all but the last digit. What does it tell you?" · "Can it go in both circles?" · "Is it odd or even? How do you know?"
  - watch: Looking at the first digit instead of the last. · Thinking numbers ending in 5 are multiples of 10. · Missing a multiple when crossing 100 (96, then 104). · The workbook uses > and < and words like "the 7th multiple" without teaching them; read them aloud as "more than", "less than" and "4 × 7".
  - away from screen: On a car journey or walk, spot house numbers or number plates and decide if they are multiples of 2, 5 or 10.

### 4. `times-ten-machine` — Times-10 Machine 🔟
- **section**: 5.1
- **summary**: Feed a number into the ×10 machine and watch each digit slide one place left on a place-value chart.
- **book**: Textbook p.76 Q6–Q8, p.88 Check your progress Q3 (8 × 10, 10 × 3, 27 × 10) · Workbook p.62 Q4, p.63 Q8, p.64 Q11–Q12
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Bundles of ten.* 4 pencils become 4 boxes of 10 pencils: 10, 20, 30, 40. "4 × 10 = 40."
  2. *The slide.* A hundreds/tens/ones chart. The child presses ×10 and the 4 slides from ones to tens; a 0 drops into the ones. Then 23: the 2 slides to hundreds, the 3 to tens, 0 in ones = 230.
  3. *Always ends in 0.* Bolt shows several results: the ones digit is always 0, so ×10 answers are always even (Q7).
- **Practice (8)**
  - Level 1: single-digit × 10 (1–9) with the boxes-of-10 picture and the chart; answer 10–90.
  - Level 2: 2-digit × 10 (10–99) by dragging digits on the chart; answers 100–990. Both orders (10 × 3 and 3 × 10).
  - Level 3: word problems (boxes of 10 pencils, packs of 10 stickers, 10p coins: 23 boxes → 230; ten 65 cm planks → 650 cm) and missing-number versions (__ × 10 = 470). One "Bolt says 0 × 10 = 10. Is Bolt right?" card: 10 empty boxes still hold nothing (Workbook p.64 Q12).
- **Mistake tags**: `added-ten`, `zero-wrong-place`, `digits-not-moved`, `added-two-zeros`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Multiply 1-digit and 2-digit numbers by 10 by moving each digit one place to the left.
  - say: "Every ones becomes a ten." · "Which column does the 2 move to?" · "What goes in the empty ones place?"
  - watch: Adding 10 instead of multiplying (23 × 10 = 33). · Putting the 0 in the wrong place. · Saying 'just add a zero' without knowing why. · Thinking 0 × 10 is 10 (the workbook tests this, but the textbook never multiplies by zero).
  - away from screen: Count 10p coins in piles: 6 coins is 60p; then try 12 coins.

### 5. `double-it-tables` — Double It! 2s, 4s, 8s 🕷️
- **section**: 5.2 (and the doubling part of 5.3)
- **summary**: Slide a copy of an array beside itself to turn a 2× fact into 4× and then 8× (and 3× into 6×).
- **book**: Textbook p.78 Q1–2 and Worked example, p.79 Q3–Q4, p.80 Q6 and Tip, p.83 Q3 · Workbook p.65 Q1–Q2, p.67 Q10, p.68 Q12, p.70 Q3
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Group size doubles.* Bicycles (2 wheels each) for 6 children: 2 × 6 = 12. Swap the bikes for cars (4 wheels): each group doubles, so 4 × 6 = 24. Bolt: "Same number of groups, twice as many in each, so the answer doubles."
  2. *See the doubling.* An array of 6 rows of 2 is mirrored to make 6 rows of 4; jumps of 4 on a number line show 4, 8, … 24.
  3. *And again.* Cars become spiders' legs (8): 8 × 6 = 48. A doubles strip (12 → 24 → 48) stays on screen.
  4. *Halving back.* Arrows go both ways: halve 4 × 9 = 36 to get 2 × 9 = 18 (only the group size and the answer halve).
- **Practice (10)**
  - Level 1: picture questions: count sides of squares (4 each) or legs of chairs and spiders; write the fact (4 × 6, 8 × 5). Number of groups 2–6.
  - Level 2: double/halve tables with one missing cell: 2× ↔ 4× facts (× 3 to × 9), and 3× ↔ 6× facts (× 5, 7, 8, 9). A tappable doubles helper shows double 14 = 28, etc.
  - Level 3: 3-column chains 2× → 4× → 8× with one fact given in any column (e.g. 8 × 5 = 40 given, find 4 × 5 and 2 × 5); products up to 72.
- **Mistake tags**: `doubled-both-numbers`, `forgot-to-double-answer`, `halved-wrong-way`, `doubling-error`, `counted-in-ones`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Use the 2 times table to work out the 4 and 8 times tables by doubling (and the 6s from the 3s).
  - say: "Only the group size doubles. The number of groups stays the same." · "What's double 18? Let's do 10 + 10 and 8 + 8." · "If 4 × 9 is 36, what is half of it?"
  - watch: Doubling both numbers (2 × 6 → 4 × 12). · Getting the double of a 2-digit number wrong (double 36). · Counting every leg one by one.
  - away from screen: Count chair legs around the table (4 each), then pretend each chair is a spider (8 legs): how many now?

### 6. `split-and-add-tables` — Split and Add (3s, 6s, 9s) 🁣
- **section**: 5.3
- **summary**: Make the 9× and other tricky facts by splitting an array into two easy parts and adding them.
- **book**: Textbook p.82 Q1–Q2, p.84 Q5–Q6, p.85 Q8 (dominoes), p.86 Q9, p.88 Check your progress Q3 · Workbook p.69 Q1, p.70 Q2 and Q4, p.71 Q6 and Q8, p.72 Q9, p.73 Q10 and Q12
- **Learn screens**
  1. *3s to 6s.* An array of 4 rows of 3 (cartons of 3 yoghurts). Copy it: 4 rows of 6. "3 × 4 = 12, so 6 × 4 = 24."
  2. *Doubling 6 overshoots 9.* Bolt doubles 6 and gets 12, not 9. "Try adding instead: 6 + 3 = 9."
  3. *Split the domino.* Dominoes with 5 and 4 spots: 3 dominoes = 9 × 3. Drag a split line between the 5s and 4s: 5 × 3 = 15 and 4 × 3 = 12, total 27.
  4. *Connected facts.* 6 × 9 = 54 sits in the middle of a web; the child drags out 9 × 6, 54 ÷ 9, 54 ÷ 6, half (3 × 9 = 27) and double (12 × 9).
- **Practice (10)**
  - Level 1: complete 3× and 6× rows (× 1 to × 10) on a counting stick, tapping each jump; then match 3× and 6× facts with the same answer (3 × 4 = 6 × 2).
  - Level 2: split arrays: an array of up to 9 groups of 3–9 with a draggable split line; fill "__ × __ + __ × __ = __" (e.g. 9 × 3 = 5 × 3 + 4 × 3, 8 × 4 = 5 × 4 + 3 × 4 = 32, 9 × 6 = 6 × 6 + 3 × 6 = 54).
  - Level 3: multiplication walls (bottom bricks such as 1, 3, 1 / 9, 1, 2 / 1, 6, 1 / 3, 1, 9 / 1, 9, 1; multiply neighbours), the workbook's 3× + 6× = 9× table (including the × 0 row: 3 groups of nothing plus 6 groups of nothing is still nothing), the "10 lots minus 1 lot" route to 9× (9 × 4 = 40 − 4) shown as an array of 10 columns with one column crossed out, and a connected-facts web: from one fact choose all the true related facts from 8 options.
- **Mistake tags**: `doubled-six-for-nine`, `split-not-added`, `split-both-numbers`, `added-in-wall`, `six-seven-eight-mixup`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Build the 3, 6 and 9 times tables by doubling and adding, and split hard facts into easy parts.
  - say: "Can you split it into a 5 part and a smaller part?" · "Six lots plus three lots makes nine lots." · "You know 6 × 9 = 54. What else do you know now?"
  - watch: Doubling the 6s to try to get the 9s. · Splitting but forgetting to add the two parts. · Adding the bricks in the wall instead of multiplying. · With the 10s-minus trick, taking away just 1 instead of one for every group (the workbook's wording on p.73 is easy to misread).
  - away from screen: Play dominoes: for each domino you play, say its total and how many you'd have with three of them.

### 7. `jump-along-sequences` — Jump Along 🐸
- **section**: 5.1 Q9, 5.2 Q7–Q9, 5.3 Q7 (count in 4s, 8s, 3s, 6s, 9s from any start)
- **summary**: A frog jumps along a number line by the rule; find the next terms, the missing terms or the rule itself.
- **book**: Textbook p.71 Getting started Q3, p.77 Q9, p.80 Q7, p.81 Q8–Q9 and Think like a mathematician, p.84 Q7, p.88 Check your progress Q4 · Workbook p.66 Worked example 2 and Q5, p.68 Q11, p.69 Q16, p.70 Q5, p.73 Q11
- **Learn screens**
  1. *Rule and start.* Lily pads on a number line. Start at 5, rule "add 4": the child clicks to make the frog jump 4 each time; the terms 5, 9, 13, 17 are written above. The start is not a jump.
  2. *Odd or even?* The terms are coloured odd/even; Bolt asks why they are all odd (odd start, even jumps).
  3. *Start at the rule.* Start 9, rule add 9: the terms are exactly the 9 times table.
  4. *Find the rule.* 3, 11, 19, __, 35: measure the jump between pads (8) and fill the gaps.
- **Practice (8)**
  - Level 1: continue 4–5 terms with rules add 2, 5, 10 or 4 from any start up to 50 (and add 10 across a hundred: 174, 184, 194, …).
  - Level 2: rules add 3, 6, 8 or 9 from any start up to 50; describe the terms (tick: all even / all odd / multiples of the rule).
  - Level 3: find the rule and missing terms (e.g. 3, 11, 19, __, 35, __ or 7, 15, __, __); a "subtract" rule with the frog jumping backwards (100, 91, 82 … does it land on 1?, from Workbook p.73 Q11); open puzzle: two terms are 13 and 29, choose a rule that makes a 5-term sequence fit (add 4, add 8 or add 16 all work).
- **Mistake tags**: `counted-start-as-jump`, `wrong-rule`, `bridging-error`, `odd-even-wrong`, `one-answer-only`
- **Parent tips**
  - goal: Count on in 2s, 3s, 4s, 5s, 6s, 8s, 9s and 10s from any number, and find the rule of a sequence.
  - say: "The first number is where the frog sits. Now jump." · "How far is it from this pad to the next?" · "Will the next number be odd or even? Why?"
  - watch: Counting the start number as the first jump. · Slipping when crossing a ten (17 + 4). · Thinking there is only one right answer in the open puzzle.
  - away from screen: Hop along stairs or paving slabs counting in 3s or 4s, starting from a number other than 0.
